
There’s a pattern in history that becomes obvious once you see it, and then you can’t unsee it.

Every system of human organization eventually gets captured by whoever sits at the top. And that capture creates the conditions for the next system. It’s not a bug in history it’s the mechanism.

Hunter-gatherers had leaders, but those leaders hunted with you. They ate what you ate. If the tribe starved, the leader starved. Accountability wasn’t a principle, it was a physical fact. Power and risk were bundled together and you couldn’t separate them.

Then agriculture changed everything. Surplus meant you could store wealth. And once you can store wealth, you can concentrate it. Suddenly there’s enough abundance that people can specialize some fight, some farm, some administrate. And someone has to sit at the top of that hierarchy. The king emerged not just from ambition but from genuine need. Complex societies need coordination.

But here’s the problem with kings. Once power becomes hereditary, you’ve severed the link between competence and leadership. You’re essentially running a lottery where the winner is whoever happened to be born to the right father. This works fine when you get lucky and the son is capable. It fails catastrophically when you don’t.

Democracy was the correction. The insight behind it was simple if leaders need votes to stay in power, they have to at least partially serve the people they govern. Accountability gets reintroduced, not through physical proximity like in tribal life, but through the mechanism of elections.

It was a brilliant solution. For a while.

The thing democracy didn’t anticipate was industrialization. Industry made it possible to concentrate capital in ways that had no historical precedent. And capital, it turns out, can purchase political outcomes almost as reliably as a crown can. You don’t need to be king if you own the media, fund the campaigns, and employ the lobbyists. The form of democracy remained. The substance started evaporating.

This is roughly where we are now.

What’s interesting isn’t just the pattern but why it keeps repeating. Every system fails in the same way the people who sit at the top figure out how to insulate themselves from the accountability mechanisms that were supposed to constrain them. Kings made power hereditary. Democratic politicians and their donors made power financial. The capture always happens. The only variable is how long it takes.

The deeper question is what disruption breaks the current system? Because historically, disruption is the only thing that actually does it. Not argument, not reform, not good intentions. Disruption.

Agriculture disrupted tribalism. Industrialization disrupted agrarian monarchy. Something will disrupt the current arrangement. The candidates are obvious AI eliminating the economic leverage workers have, climate change exposing the inability of existing institutions to coordinate at the required scale, or the simple accumulated weight of inequality becoming politically unsustainable.

My bet is AI is the most interesting one. Here’s why. Every previous disruption changed what was scarce. Agriculture made food less scarce. Industry made goods less scarce. AI makes cognitive labor less scarce. And cognitive labor is what most educated people in democracies trade for their economic security and therefore their political influence. When that gets commoditized, the social contract that underlies modern democracy work hard, contribute, earn your place stops making sense.

This will create enormous pressure for a new arrangement. What that arrangement looks like depends on who moves fastest into the vacuum.

Which brings us to the early 20th century, because we’ve been here before.

The last time institutions lost this much legitimacy simultaneously after WWI, during the Depression a very specific type of person succeeded. Not the most competent. Not the most reasonable. The ones who succeeded were the ones who could read mass anxiety and offer a simple story about it.

Lenin, Hitler, FDR, Gandhi morally completely different, but structurally doing the same thing. Sensing that the old framework had lost its grip, and moving into that vacuum with a narrative. The narrative didn’t have to be complicated. It had to be emotionally true. It had to answer the question ordinary people were actually asking why is my life hard and who is responsible.

The moderates failed. They kept trying to patch the existing system, which meant they were implicitly defending it. When institutions have genuinely lost legitimacy, defending them is fatal. You look like you’re protecting the people who broke everything.

This is the warning hidden in the history.

So what kind of world are we building toward?

Probably not dramatic revolution. More likely a slow replacement democratic forms persisting while actual power concentrates further in technology platforms, capital, and AI-augmented surveillance. The frog in the gradually heating water. Most people won’t notice a single dramatic moment of change because there won’t be one.

But underneath that surface stability, pressure builds. It always does. The people who got captured at the top always overestimate how long the arrangement can hold.

The tribe machine doesn’t stop. It just resets.

And when it resets, the question is always the same one it’s been since the first hunter-gatherer stepped forward to lead a hunt

who is actually willing to share your risk?

Every system that answers that question honestly tends to work. Every system that stops answering it honestly tends to end.

We’re in the business of finding out what comes next.
