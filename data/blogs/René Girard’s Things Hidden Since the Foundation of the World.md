
René Girard’s Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World

René Girard’s Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World is one of those rare books that tries to explain almost everything at once: religion, violence, psychology, culture, and the Bible, all through a single set of ideas. At the center are three claims:

Human desire is imitative (mimetic).

Imitation easily escalates into rivalry and collective violence (the scapegoat mechanism).

The Judaeo‑Christian scriptures gradually reveal—rather than hide—this mechanism, culminating in the Gospels’ focus on the innocent victim.

What follows is a blog‑friendly walkthrough of those ideas and why they matter now.

Mimetic Desire: We Copy Not Just Behavior, But Wanting Itself
Girard starts from something deceptively simple: humans are brilliant imitators. We don’t just copy gestures or language; we copy what others value and desire.

When someone wants something, that alone makes it more desirable to others.

Desire spreads by imitation: I want it because you want it, not because of its intrinsic value.

He calls this acquisitive mimesis. A classic example: put identical toys in a room with children. They will often fight over one toy, not because it’s unique, but because each is attracted by the other’s desire. The object becomes a signal of someone else’s wanting—and that is what hooks us.

This leads to mimetic rivalry: the more two people imitate each other’s desire, the more they collide over the same thing, escalating conflict.

From Rivalry to Social Meltdown: The Mimetic Crisis
Humans, unlike animals, don’t have rigid instinctual boundaries to stop rivalry from spiraling. So when mimetic desires start converging, things can get chaotic:

Differences (status, roles, identities) begin to blur.

Everyone feels like everyone else’s mirror and enemy.

Violence becomes reciprocal and contagious.

Girard calls this a mimetic crisis. It’s like a social feedback loop: each person’s aggression intensifies the next person’s, until the whole group teeters on collapse.

Traditional societies, Girard argues, intuited this danger. That’s why they built dense systems of prohibitions (taboos)—around sex, property, status, and access to key resources—to pre‑emptively block situations where mimetic rivalry would explode. Many religious “don’ts” are less about abstract morality and more about crisis‑prevention.

The Scapegoat Mechanism: How Violence Produces Order
Girard’s most famous and controversial claim is about what happens when norms fail and a mimetic crisis goes full‑blown. At the height of chaos, something eerie tends to happen:

The crowd converges on a single victim—someone marginal, different, vulnerable, or ambiguously marked.

The group decides—often implicitly, not consciously—that this one person is the cause of their troubles.

They expel, kill, or symbolically destroy this victim.

The result? Peace. Unity. The fighting stops.

Because calm follows the victim’s elimination, the community interprets the victim as both monstrous (cause of crisis) and miraculous (source of restored order). This is the scapegoat (victimage) mechanism.

For Girard, this is the buried engine behind archaic religion and culture:

Myths retell the crisis and killing but from the persecutors’ point of view, portraying the victim as guilty or semi‑divine and justifying the violence.

Rituals and sacrifices re‑enact the founding murder in controlled, repeatable ways—substituting animals, ritual kings, symbolic offerings—to renew social cohesion without falling into uncontrolled bloodshed.

Prohibitions work upstream to reduce the chances of another uncontrollable mimetic meltdown.

Culture, in this picture, is founded on a misrecognized murder: the community’s violence is projected onto a sacred victim who carries both their guilt and their peace.

Religion Reframed: Not Speculation, But Violence Management
This flips the usual picture of religion.

Instead of seeing religion as primitive metaphysics or superstition, Girard treats it as an early—and surprisingly effective—technology for managing internal violence:

Sacrifice channels aggression onto contained victims.

Ritual repetition keeps the founding trauma at a safe symbolic distance.

Myth provides a narrative that stabilizes and justifies this system.

Religion works because it does something practical: it stops communities from destroying themselves through mimetic rivalry. But it works by lying about the victim—by presenting the scapegoat as genuinely guilty or legitimately sacrificed.

Myth vs. the Bible: Who Gets to Tell the Victim’s Story?
A huge part of Things Hidden is Girard’s reading of the Judaeo‑Christian scriptures alongside world mythologies.

How Myth Talks
Myths describe crises (plagues, wars, monsters, social breakdown) resolved when a figure—often a god, king, or monster—is killed, expelled, or dismembered.

The narrative sides with the community and implies the victim really was dangerous or guilty.

The innocence of the victim is concealed; the community’s violence is sacralized.

How the Bible Talks
Girard argues that the Bible gradually switches perspective from persecutors to victims:

Joseph is sold by his brothers, falsely accused, imprisoned, then unexpectedly becomes savior of his family and Egypt.

Job is attacked by friends who insist he must be guilty, while the text insists on his innocence.

The “suffering servant” in Isaiah is despised and rejected, yet is portrayed as innocent and bearing others’ sins.

These stories expose, rather than bless, the community’s tendency to blame and expel a victim. The persecutors’ voice is present but questioned or overturned.

The Bible, for Girard, is a long, uneven but real process of demystifying the scapegoat mechanism—slowly revealing that victims are often innocent and collective violence is a lie.

The Gospels: The Founding Murder Exposed
The turning point is the Gospel passion narratives.

They contain all the structural elements of the scapegoat scenario:

Rising tension and confusion in the community.

Political and religious authorities afraid of unrest.

A crowd stirred up, choosing one man to bear the blame.

A formal but unjust trial, followed by execution.

If this were a myth, the story would justify the killing and either divinize or demonize the victim in a way that reinforces the communal narrative. But the Gospels systematically insist:

Jesus is innocent.

The charges are fabricated.

The crowd is manipulated.

The authorities know they are sacrificing an innocent man.

Even more radically, the resurrected Jesus doesn’t return seeking revenge or affirming the persecutors’ story. He returns forgiving them and exposing the lie of sacrificial violence.

When Jesus quotes “things hidden since the foundation of the world,” Girard takes that to mean: the founding murders and scapegoat mechanisms that have silently structured culture from the beginning are now being unveiled as human, not divine, acts.

Is the Cross a Sacrifice or a Revelation Against Sacrifice?
Historically, many Christian theologies have interpreted Jesus’ death as a sacrifice demanded by God to satisfy divine justice or wrath.

Girard pushes hard against this. If God “needs” a victim, then God is just another mythic deity running on the sacrificial logic the Gospels are trying to expose.

Instead, he proposes a non‑sacrificial reading:

The cross is not God’s demand, but humanity’s scapegoating at work again.

God doesn’t orchestrate the violence; God exposes it, forgives it, and breaks its spell by taking the victim’s side.

The “sacrifice” is human—not divine—violence, revealed and judged in the light of the resurrection.

On this reading, genuine Christianity is anti‑sacrificial: it calls for solidarity with victims, refusal of scapegoating, and dismantling of social orders that rely on exclusion and persecution.

When Christianity Betrays Its Own Revelation
Girard isn’t naïve about church history. He acknowledges that “Christian” institutions have often reverted to sacrificial logic:

Persecution of heretics, Jews, “witches,” and others.

Theologies that picture the Father as needing to be appeased by the Son’s blood.

Political projects that sacralize violence in the name of Christian identity.

In those moments, Christianity behaves like myth: it hides its own violence by projecting guilt onto others. Yet, Girard insists, the texts themselves retain an anti‑sacrificial edge that keeps destabilizing these betrayals from within.

Over centuries, this biblical “bias toward victims” contributes to:

Modern human rights discourse.

Suspicion of political and religious violence.

The moral centrality of the marginalized.

But the flip side is that we’re now hyper‑sensitive to victims and still deeply mimetic—creating new scapegoats in the name of protecting victims.

Logos Reimagined: From Greek Rationality to the Crucified Word
In one of the more philosophical parts, Girard compares Heraclitus’ and other Greek philosophers’ concept of the logos with the Gospel of John’s “In the beginning was the Word (Logos).”

Greek logos: the rational structure of reality, a cosmic law of becoming and order, sometimes linked to conflict and balance.

Johannine Logos: not just an abstract principle, but a person—the Word made flesh, rejected and crucified.

Girard argues that the true rational structure of the world is unveiled not in a neutral cosmic law but in the story of the victim:

The Logos reveals that human order has been built on scapegoating.

True divine order is not founded on violence but revealed through the innocent victim who forgives and unmasks that violence.

So John’s Logos is a radical critique of all systems (religious or philosophical) that are comfortable with sacrificial foundations.

Interdividual Psychology: Desire Is Always Triangular
In Book III, Girard turns from anthropology to psychology, but keeps the same mimetic lens.

He argues that human desire is triangular:

Subject: the one who desires.

Model/Mediator: the one whose desire is imitated.

Object: the thing apparently desired.

We don’t usually desire objects directly; we desire them through models.

When the model is distant or unattainable (a celebrity, hero, saint), Girard calls it external mediation—less likely to lead to direct rivalry.

When the model is close (a colleague, sibling, friend), it becomes internal mediation—fertile ground for jealousy, competition, and conflict.

This triangular structure helps explain:

Career envy (“I want that job because he has it”).

Romantic jealousy (two people desiring the same person because each sees the other’s desire).

Social comparison on steroids in modern life.

Desire is not a straight line from self to object but a triangle, and the most explosive tensions live on the line between self and model.

Modernity: Liberation or New Forms of Slavery?
Traditional religious cultures constrained desire with taboos, initiations, and rigid status differences. Modern societies tore many of those down in the name of freedom and authenticity:

“Be yourself.”

“Follow your desire.”

“Liberate yourself from repression.”

Girard’s critique is sharp: when you remove external constraints and differences, you don’t get pure, innocent desire; you get more mimetic rivalry.

External obstacles (laws, taboos, hierarchies) recede.

Internal obstacles (other desiring subjects, rivals) grow more intense.

Individuals feel more “free,” yet are more trapped in endless comparison and competition: professionally, sexually, socially.

The modern world is incredibly creative and productive precisely because it unleashes mimetic desire—but it’s also saturated with anxiety, status panic, and the constant search for new scapegoats to blame when systems crack.

Girard vs. Freud, Marx, Nietzsche: Who Is the Real Scapegoat?
Girard sees Freud, Marx, Nietzsche, and other modern masters of suspicion as having done something important: they all try to unmask hidden mechanisms of domination and repression.

But he also thinks they fall into a trap: each theory designates its own scapegoat.

Freud: the father, the law, the superego, Oedipal structures.

Marx: the bourgeoisie, capitalists, ruling classes.

Nietzsche: slave morality, ressentiment, the weak who resent the strong.

Each system channels resentment toward a particular guilty group—yet that is precisely what the scapegoat mechanism does. They “unmask” some sacrificial mechanisms while still relying on new victims to explain and resolve conflict.

Girard’s alternative is more radical: no scapegoats at all. Everyone is implicated in mimetic violence; no group can claim pure innocence over others. That’s a harder, less comforting truth.

Double Binds, Doubles, and Psychopathology
Drawing on Gregory Bateson’s idea of the double bind, Girard shows how mimetic situations can psychologically crush people:

The disciple is told, “Imitate me, be like me” (master, parent, mentor).

But when imitation becomes too successful, the model flips: “Don’t rival me, don’t surpass me.”

This generates a contradictory command: “Be like me—but don’t be like me.” The disciple internalizes this conflict and starts to split internally, creating an inner double: a self that admires and a self that hates the model.

Girard suggests that:

Many symptoms labeled neuroses or psychoses arise from extreme versions of these mimetic entanglements.

Hallucinated doubles, persecution delusions, obsessive rivalry can be read as mimetic structures, not just mysterious inner drives.

Crucially, he insists there’s continuity between “normal” and “pathological” desire; both operate on the same mimetic logic, differing mainly in intensity and breakdown of external stabilizers.

Sex, Sadism, Masochism, Homosexuality: Desire’s Paradoxes
Girard applies the same framework to sexuality.

Sadism and masochism are not special sexual instincts but theatrical crystallizations of mimetic rivalry.

The subject becomes fixated on the rival’s power to grant or deny access to the object, then begins to eroticize that power and violence.

In some cases, the rival fully replaces the original object: the subject’s main focus becomes the same‑sex model–rival.

Girard interprets certain forms of homosexuality this way—as advanced phases of mimetic desire where the model has become the object.

He also notes ritualized homosexuality and cannibalism in some traditional cultures, where the appetite (for sex or food) shifts from an external object to the mimetic partner. For him, that’s another sign that mimesis, not libido in isolation, is the deeper driver.

You may or may not agree with these readings, but they show how far Girard is willing to push a unified mimetic theory across domains typically fragmented in modern psychology.

Desire Without Object: Chasing “Being” Instead of Things
As rivalry intensifies, the actual object (money, partner, job, status symbol) matters less. What really matters is the being thought to reside in the rival:

“He has something I lack.”

“If I had what he has, I would finally be someone.”

“The object is just the gateway to his kind of existence.”

Girard calls this metaphysical or ontological desire: wanting not just things, but the seemingly superior being of the model. This explains why:

People can win what they wanted and still feel empty.

Rivals begin to resemble each other more and more; they become doubles.

Social and personal conflicts can spiral even when material stakes are small.

At this stage, desire is “without object” in a strict sense; the object is just a pretext. The true focus is the other person’s perceived being.

Beyond Scandal: Can We Escape the Trap?
The Greek word skandalon (stumbling block, offense) becomes a key category for Girard: it is the figure or obstacle that both fascinates and enrages, the rival who blocks our path yet defines our desire.

To go “beyond scandal” means:

Recognizing the mimetic nature of our desires.

Seeing how we are drawn to and trapped by rivals and scapegoats.

Refusing both pride (“I am innocent”) and self‑loathing (“I alone am guilty”), and instead acknowledging shared complicity.

Girard argues that this break cannot happen by sheer willpower within the mimetic system itself. It requires a new model who does not rival us—a non‑rivalrous model whose desire does not block but liberates.

For Girard, that model is the crucified and risen Christ: the innocent victim who exposes scapegoating and offers a pattern of non‑violent, forgiving mimesis. The goal is not to stop imitating, but to imitate differently:

From competitive models to compassionate ones.

From identities formed against victims to identities formed around reconciliation.

Why Girard’s Insights Hit Home Today
Reading Things Hidden in 2026, Girard’s framework feels almost eerily relevant:

Social Media and Envy

Platforms are engines of mimetic desire: everyone displays what they want and have, inviting imitation and rivalry.

Algorithms amplify visibility of “models,” intensifying comparison and internal obstacles.

Politics and Culture Wars

Groups increasingly define themselves by shared enemies.

Outrage cycles often follow scapegoat dynamics: complex crises are simplified into “It’s their fault,” and purges feel cleansing.

Victim Discourse

Being a victim now carries moral authority, which is good—but it also tempts groups to compete for victim status and to scapegoat others as “oppressors.”

Girard helps explain how even “pro‑victim” politics can slide into new forms of persecution.

Mental Health

Rising anxiety, depression, and burnout are often tangled with comparisons, rivalry, and the feeling of being perpetually “behind.”

A mimetic lens invites questions like: “Who is my model here? Whose desire am I copying? What rivalry is structuring this pain?”

Takeaways You Can Apply Personally
To translate Girard’s dense theory into personal practice, a few questions are powerful:

Whose desire is shaping mine? When you want something intensely (job, lifestyle, relationship), ask: “Would I still want this if I had never seen that person want it?”

Where am I in a double bind? Are there people who simultaneously tell you to “be like me” and “don’t threaten me”? Noticing this helps you stop blaming yourself for an impossible situation.

Who is my scapegoat? When life feels chaotic, who do you instinctively think “If they were gone, things would be fine”? That’s your candidate scapegoat. Girard’s warning: this is where illusion is strongest.

Who are my models now? You cannot stop imitating—but you can choose better models: people who do not need you to fail, who do not live by exclusion, who are not obsessed with enemies.
